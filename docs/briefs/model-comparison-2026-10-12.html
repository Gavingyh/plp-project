Which FinBERT should the dashboard and chatbot use?

Six versions of the sentiment model were scored on the same held-out sentences. The best is FinBERT first adapted to the project's own text, then fine-tuned on labels made by an LLM. Its overall accuracy is about the same as plain FinBERT's. What changes is the kind of mistake: it stops calling almost everything neutral, and finds three in five negative sentences where plain FinBERT found one in five.

What was compared

Every version starts from the same published model, FinBERT, which was trained on financial news. They differ in two ways: which labelled sentences they were fine-tuned on, and whether FinBERT first read the project's own sentences without labels.

Two terms. Fine-tuning shows the model sentences with known answers. Adapting comes before that and uses no answers at all: FinBERT keeps doing its original exercise, guessing hidden words, on the project's Reddit posts, news and reports, so it meets local product names and Reddit's style before it is asked to judge sentiment.

The 1,563 training sentences are 917 from the first labelling round, 275 from broker research, 240 added for thin topics and 131 that earlier models were least sure about. The LLM labelled 616 positive, 586 neutral and 361 negative. They are LLM labels and are reported as such.

How it was tested

The test sentences

  • 299 rows from the first round, set aside before any training and labelled by people: 166 neutral, 94 positive, 39 negative.
  • 100 rows from broker research, labelled blind by a person: 42 positive, 35 neutral, 23 negative.
  • No test sentence, and no near-copy of one, is among the training rows. The adapting step left out 2,270 labelled sentences and 752 more that resembled one.

The rules

  • Three runs each. Every fine-tuned model was trained three times from different random starts (seeds 42, 1 and 7), because one run's score can be luck at this size.
  • The measure is macro-F1. It averages the score for negative, neutral and positive, so the rare negative class counts as much as the common neutral one. Accuracy alone rewards a model for calling everything neutral: 56% of the test rows are neutral.
  • The rule for choosing was fixed first: highest test macro-F1 over the three seeds, with cross-validation as the tie-breaker. Nothing was tuned on the test rows.

The headline: each step up is small, and they add up

On the 299 first-round test rows, base FinBERT scores 0.571. Fine-tuning on people's labels lifts that to 0.604 and, once retrained, 0.621. Switching to LLM labels, with more rows, gives 0.631. Adapting the encoder first gives 0.649.

Against base FinBERT the gain is clear. 0.649 against 0.571 is a gap of 4 times the spread between seeds (0.018). Every one of the three adapted runs is above every earlier model's mean.

Between neighbours the gaps are small. The best set leads the one below it by 0.018, about one seed's spread. No single step is a sure thing by itself; the order holds because other measures agree, as the later sections show.

Averaging three models helps. Letting the three adapted runs vote gives 0.670, and that averaged model is what the product would use.

Accuracy barely moves. The kind of error does.

Base FinBERT gets 209 of the 299 rows right; the averaged adapted model gets 214. Row by row, the adapted model fixes 41 sentences that base FinBERT got wrong and breaks 36 it had right. A difference that small could be chance (exact test, p = 0.65). So this is not a story about more correct answers.

It is a story about which answers. The two tables count every test sentence by what it truly is and what the model called it.

Base FinBERT answers neutral 221 times out of 299. That is safe when most rows are neutral, but it finds only 8 of the 39 negative sentences and 50 of the 94 positive ones.

The adapted model finds 23 of the 39 negatives and 69 of the 94 positives. The price is 44 neutral sentences now called positive or negative, up from 15.

For a sentiment index this is the better trade. A model that hides four in five negative sentences cannot show a bank's sentiment falling. One that is sometimes too quick to call a sentence positive or negative can be reined in with a threshold, which is the last step before the scores are stored.

Precision and recall by class

Recall is the share of the truly negative (or neutral, or positive) sentences the model found. Precision is the share of its negative calls that were right. Figures are the mean of three seeds on the 299 first-round rows.

Negative recall is where the models separate: 0.21, 0.41, 0.49 and 0.61, in the order they were built.

Negative precision stays near a half for all of them (0.48 to 0.53): about one negative call in two is wrong. With only 39 negative test rows this figure is the least stable on the page.

Base FinBERT's neutral recall of 0.91 is the other side of its caution. The fine-tuned models give up some of it (0.71 for the adapted set) to find more of the other two classes.

Does it hold on other text?

The first-round rows are mostly Reddit and earnings reports. The broker research rows are analysts' prose, closer to what FinBERT was first trained on, and base FinBERT is strong there: fine-tuning on people's labels never beat it. The adapted model on LLM labels does.

On these rows the averaged adapted model is right on 14 sentences base FinBERT misses and wrong on 6 it gets (p = 0.12); against plain FinBERT fine-tuned on the same labels it is 10 against 2 (p = 0.039). On all 399 test rows together, macro-F1 is 0.685 against base FinBERT's 0.648.

The gain is on formal text: earnings reports (0.73 to 0.81) and broker research (0.69 to 0.77).

Reddit does not improve on accuracy (0.70 to 0.68). Most Reddit rows are neutral, so base FinBERT's habit of answering neutral scores well there. Reddit is 184 of the 399 test rows and remains the weak source.

Is adapting worth it, or is plain FinBERT enough?

This is the closest call on the page, so it was measured a second way. The training rows were split into five folds, and each fold was held out in turn while a model trained on the other four. That was done for three seeds, from plain FinBERT and from the adapted encoder on identical folds, so each pair differs only in the starting point.

The adapted encoder wins 11 of 15 pairs, by +0.014 on average (0.686 to 0.700). A paired test puts the true gain between +0.003 and +0.025 (t = 2.75, p = 0.016). Small, and not zero.

The gain was larger on 6 Oct (+0.038 on 978 rows). With 1,563 labelled rows plain FinBERT has caught up part of the way, which is what adapting is expected to do: it helps most when labels are scarce.

It costs little to keep. Adapting took about 1 hour 40 minutes, once. The model the product runs is the same size and speed either way.

Why not switch on every tuning technique?

The chosen model already stacks every technique that passed testing. The others were tried one at a time, then together, and left out because they did not beat the noise. Each was tested by cross-validation on the training rows, never on the test rows.

In the chosen model

  • Adapting the encoder on the project's own text
  • Fine-tuning on labelled sentences
  • The larger label set: 1,563 rows, up from 978
  • 131 rows the earlier models were least sure about
  • The topic given to the model as a readable name
  • Longer training that stops when the held-out score stops rising
  • Averaging three trained models

Tried and left out

  • Balanced class weights: make a mistake on a rare class cost more.
  • Weights by source: make the training mix of Reddit, news and reports match the corpus.
  • Class weights matching the corpus's mix: lean the model toward the share of neutral sentences in the whole corpus.
  • The helpful ones all together.

The weighting techniques sit inside the noise. Balanced class weights gained +0.009 and source weights +0.012, when two runs of the very same setup differ by about 0.024.

Switching them all on did not add them up. Together they gained +0.014, no more than the best one alone (+0.016). Several of them correct the same weakness, so the second has little left to correct.

One made it worse. Weights matching the corpus's mix cost −0.096: they pushed the model back toward calling everything neutral, the fault fine-tuning is there to cure.

The readable topic name is kept, and credited with nothing. Its first result (+0.016) shrank to +0.005 when repeated on three seeds. It stays because the models were built with it and nothing shows it does harm.

Adapting the encoder is the only addition that held up twice: +0.038 with 978 rows and +0.014 with 1,563, better in most folds both times.

Why not add them anyway? With about 1,600 labelled rows, gains this small cannot be told apart from luck. Adding techniques until the test score rises would be fitting the test rows, and the score would stop meaning anything.

One gap. The weighting techniques were tested on plain FinBERT with people's labels, not on the adapted encoder with LLM labels. A run with everything switched on would close that gap; it is expected to land within noise of the chosen model.

What it would do to the index

The dashboard does not show sentences; it shows a balance of positive against negative. So a second question matters: when the labels on the test rows are added up, how close does each model's total come to the truth?

On the first-round rows the adapted model is closer. The labels balance at +0.184; base FinBERT reads +0.161 and the adapted model +0.174. Base FinBERT also calls only 26% of rows positive or negative when 44% are, so its index moves less than the text does.

On the broker research rows it is further away. The labels balance at +0.190; base FinBERT reads +0.210 and the adapted model +0.090, because it calls 30 sentences negative where 23 are.

This is what the neutral threshold is for. Before the earlier fine-tuned model was proposed for the product, a margin was chosen on held-out training rows: when the model's top two answers are close, the sentence is called neutral. The same step has not been done for this model yet, and it comes before the re-score.

How sure this is, and the limits

  • One run of three seeds. The gaps between fine-tuned models are about the size of the spread between seeds. The order is trusted because the first-round rows, the broker research rows and cross-validation all agree, not because any one gap is large.
  • The test labels and the training labels follow different rules. The first-round test rows were labelled by people under the older rule for retail products, with a model's suggestion in view. The training rows were labelled by an LLM under the rules of 10 Oct. The models still score higher on the people's labels than those trained on people's labels did.
  • Few negative test rows. 39 in the first round and 23 in broker research. Negative precision and recall move by two or three points when a single row changes.
  • Cross-validation is scored against the LLM's labels, the test rows against people's. They answer slightly different questions and happen to agree.
  • An earlier adapted model is left out. The one from 6 and 7 Oct scored 0.751 on the broker research rows, but its adapting step had read sentences related to test sentences. This run fixed that; its like-for-like figure is 0.770 for the three averaged.
  • Reddit is not solved. Accuracy there is 0.68, the same as before.

Decision and what happens next

The model for the dashboard and chatbot is the adapted encoder fine-tuned on LLM labels, three runs averaged. It leads on both test sets and in cross-validation, by the rule fixed before the run.

  1. Choose the neutral threshold for the averaged model on held-out training rows, and check the index it would produce against the labels.
  2. Rehearse the re-score on a copy of the database with this model and the LLM's aspects, and show what each bank's figures become.
  3. Run the re-score once, on the owner's go-ahead, after the dashboard filters on one model version.
  4. Re-run every evaluation on the new database, including the comparison of the two aspect methods.
