1Business problem

Singapore's retail investors want to own local shares but lack research they can read quickly. This project gives them a regularly updated view of what is being said about the three local banks, topic by topic, with the sentences behind every number.

The need is documented. In the SIAS and Beansprout Pulse Survey (March 2025, 1,011 respondents), over 80% of investors remained interested in Singapore stocks, yet 24% named a lack of guidance or research as a barrier and 19% unfamiliarity. Among those with a small Singapore allocation, 31% gave unfamiliarity as the main reason for staying out, and 81% read an analyst report before investing. In an MDRT survey (April 2025, 2,000 Singapore adults), 34% rated their own financial literacy good and 15% excellent. Investors depend on analyst reports that are scarce and paywalled.

DBS, OCBC and UOB are where most retail portfolios start: together they make up roughly half of the Straits Times Index. A reader comparing them wants to know which bank is viewed better on dividends, on asset quality or on customer service, whether that view is changing, and what was said. A single sentiment score per bank answers none of these. The project therefore builds an aspect-based index: sentiment per bank and per topic, from public text, served through an API, a dashboard and a chatbot that answers from the same store and cites its evidence.

2Datasets

2.1The corpus

The corpus is public text about the three banks: 28,852 documents split into 45,844 sentences, dated 6 January 2020 to 25 September 2026. It was collected in batches and then frozen, with two late additions: 25 broker research reports, and 825 Reddit posts and comments that an earlier collection had lost. A few of the earlier evaluations cited below were run before those additions, on 44,345 sentences. The sentence is the unit of analysis throughout, because one post often praises one thing and complains about another.

Three properties of this corpus shape how every result must be read.

  • It is two kinds of language. Reddit is 56% of the sentences and is informal, personal and often about a bank's products. Earnings documents are 27% and are written by the banks about themselves. A model or an index that suits one may fail on the other, so results are reported by source as well as in total.
  • The banks are covered unequally. DBS has 24,978 sentences, UOB 13,092 and OCBC 7,774. DBS also has about six times the earnings text of either other bank (9,093 sentences against 1,561 and 1,600), and earnings text is far more positive than Reddit. A blended score therefore flatters the bank with the most self-written text, which is why the index keeps a separate figure for each source beside the blend.
  • News volume rises towards the present, because Google News favours recent articles. Trends in news counts are an artefact of collection, not of events.

Collection used only free, public endpoints. No paywalled content was taken, no vendor's sentiment score was used as a label, and UOB's earnings files were taken from the Wayback Machine instead of working around its site's bot protection.

The test labels are people's. The 299 first-round test sentences (166 neutral, 94 positive, 39 negative) and the 100 broker research test sentences (42 positive, 35 neutral, 23 negative) carry the labels people gave them, and no test sentence, or near-copy of one, is in any training set. On the first round the two annotators agreed on sentiment at Cohen's kappa 0.93 over the 1,277 sentences. That figure is an upper bound: both saw a local model's suggested sentiment, which the final labels match on 91.6% of rows, so some of the agreement is agreement with the suggestion. The test labels were not made independently of that suggestion either, which favours any model resembling the suggester. Neither FinBERT nor the fine-tuned model is that model, so the comparisons in section 4.1 stand, but the first-round test set cannot be called blind. The same holds for the broker research training rows, where the one annotator kept an LLM's suggested sentiment on 94% of rows; the 100 broker research test rows were labelled with no suggestion in view.

The training labels are an LLM's. Part-way through the project the team changed two labelling rules: a retail product's feature now carries sentiment toward the bank (a fee is negative, a bonus rate positive) where it had been neutral, and the wealth aspect was narrowed to the priority and private banking tiers. Relabelling by hand was not affordable, so a large language model labelled all 2,075 sentences under the new rules, blind to every other label: 757 positive, 879 neutral, 439 negative, with 134 flagged as not about the bank. After removing the test rows, the flagged rows and one near-copy of a test sentence, 1,563 remain for training (616 positive, 586 neutral, 361 negative).

How far can those labels be trusted? On the 1,277 first-round sentences the LLM rater agrees with people on the aspect at kappa 0.80 and on sentiment at kappa 0.79, and every sentiment disagreement found in the first comparison sat on the neutral boundary. That is close to, but below, what the two human annotators reached with each other. Section 4.1 tests the consequence directly: models trained on these labels are scored against people's labels.

3Solution approach

3.1The pipeline

Text passes through seven steps, each storing its output so that any step can be re-run alone:

  1. Collect from each source
  2. Clean and split into sentences
  3. Attribute each sentence to a bank
  4. Assign an aspect
  5. Score sentiment
  6. Aggregate into an index
  7. Serve through an API, a dashboard and a chatbot

The module requires at least two text-mining tasks and one self-built or fine-tuned deep neural network. This system has four tasks (sections 3.2 to 3.5); the neural network is the sentiment classifier of section 3.5.

3.2Task 1: discovering the aspects

The ten aspects come partly from the proposal and partly from the data. Sentence embeddings from FinBERT were reduced with UMAP and clustered with HDBSCAN, which gave 338 clusters, with 65% of sentences left unclustered. Each cluster was given a label from its most distinctive terms and sample sentences, and the labels were merged into ten aspects plus "other": asset quality, capital and liquidity, dividend policy, net interest margin, management guidance, income and results, the wealth business, investor and stock sentiment, retail banking, and the macro environment. Five are the proposal's aspects, broadened; the others, including the two largest, retail banking and investor and stock sentiment, were not in the proposal and came from the clusters. The cluster labels were drafted by an AI chat assistant under a teammate's direction and were not checked by a second person (declared in section 8).

How far does the data support that taxonomy? A later sweep of the minimum cluster size gives two answers. At a size of 10 the corpus breaks into 358 clusters, too small to name one by one, and at 25 and 50 it collapses into one cluster holding nearly every sentence, so the method does not hand over a taxonomy by itself. At 100 it gives 25 readable clusters whose structure resembles the ten aspects, which is independent support for them, with 52% of sentences left unclustered. The taxonomy is therefore a judgement informed by the clusters and later corroborated by them, not an output of the algorithm.

Sources: docs/evidence/aspect-taxonomy-2026-09-25.md, docs/evidence/aspect-discovery-sweep-2026-09-30.md.

3.3Task 2: assigning an aspect to each sentence

Two methods were built and compared, and the comparison is itself a result (section 4.2). The first assigns a sentence to every aspect whose seed sentences it resembles, by cosine similarity of FinBERT embeddings above 0.65. The second asks a local language model, llama3.1:8b, to choose exactly one aspect from a fixed prompt: a role, the task, the eleven definitions, the constraint to answer in a set format, and one invented example per aspect.

The design choice between them is about what an aspect score means. Similarity matching gave each sentence 6.4 aspects on average, so every aspect's score was close to a copy of the whole corpus and the breakdown told the reader little. One aspect per sentence makes the aspects separate, at the price of small samples in the rarer ones. The stored corpus uses the language model's aspects.

3.4Task 3: recognising which bank a sentence is about

A sentence must be attributed to the right bank, and names mislead: "UOB Kay Hian" is a separate brokerage and not the bank, "DBS Vickers" is a DBS subsidiary and counts as DBS, "POSB" is DBS, and "Bank of Singapore" and "Great Eastern" belong to OCBC. The baseline matches a list of aliases. The recogniser uses spaCy's pretrained named-entity model with a rule layer in front and a gazetteer behind, and resolves each organisation it finds to DBS, OCBC, UOB or "not the bank".

State this plainly in the final text

The recogniser is built, tested and evaluated (section 4.3), but the stored corpus is still attributed by alias matching: no step re-tags the 45,844 stored sentences. Either apply it before the figures freeze, which changes which bank some sentences count for, or say in the report that it is evaluated as a component and not yet used by the index. An examiner will ask.

3.5Task 4: sentiment classification, the fine-tuned network

The classifier reads a sentence together with the readable name of its aspect ("Dividend Policy") and returns positive, neutral or negative. It is FinBERT, a BERT model pretrained on financial text, adapted in two stages.

  • Domain-adaptive pretraining. FinBERT's masked-word training was continued for three epochs on 42,822 of the corpus's sentences, so that the encoder sees forum language and local product names before it sees any label. A leakage filter kept out every labelled sentence and anything resembling one: 2,270 labelled sentences, 37 with identical text, 147 containing or contained in one, and 568 sharing most of their words.
  • Fine-tuning. The adapted encoder was trained on the 1,563 LLM-labelled pairs for up to eight epochs with early stopping, from three random seeds, and the three models' probabilities are averaged.

Two decisions were fixed before the test rows were scored, so the test result could not influence them. The configuration was chosen by five-fold cross-validation, and the rule for choosing between candidate models was written down first: test macro-F1 over three seeds, cross-validation as the tie-breaker. A third was examined a day after the test scores were known, but without using them: the threshold for calling a sentence neutral was chosen on 601 held-out training rows, where it came out at zero, so the model's most likely class is used as it is.

Source: docs/evidence/model-rerun-2026-10-11.md. Baselines: a lexicon (VADER), TF-IDF with logistic regression, and FinBERT with no fine-tuning.

3.6The sentiment index

The index turns labels into one number per bank, aspect, source and time window, between -1 (all negative) and +1 (all positive). Each sentence counts +1, 0 or -1, weighted by the model's confidence and by its source: a news sentence counts 1.5 times a Reddit comment or an earnings sentence, because one article reaches more readers than one comment. The result is multiplied by n/(n+5), which pulls a score from few sentences towards zero, and any cell with fewer than five sentences is reported as insufficient data.

Two choices keep the index honest about its own limits. A headline figure counts each sentence once, however it is filed. And the 7-day and 30-day windows end at the newest stored sentence, 25 September 2026, not at today's date, so a corpus that has stopped growing still has a recent reading, labelled "as of" that date.

3.7The conversational interface, the API and the dashboard

The chatbot answers questions about the three banks from the index and the stored sentences, and is built so that it cannot quietly make things up. A language model (llama3.1:8b, run locally) chooses among six tools: a bank's sentiment, a comparison of the three, a trend, data coverage, a search for evidence sentences, and analyst consensus. Three layers of code sit around the model.

  • Before the model: an intent classifier sends requests for investment advice, questions about other banks, small talk, prompt injection and off-topic questions to fixed replies, so the model never sees them.
  • After the model, on quotes: any quoted passage of three or more words that no search in the conversation returned is removed, with its citation.
  • After the model, on figures: every number in the answer is compared with the tools' results, and one that matches nothing is marked in the answer.

The reasoning behind this design came from measurement, described in section 4.5: with instructions alone a third of answers cited evidence that was never retrieved, and a check in code ended that. A REST API serves every figure to any front end and rejects invalid input, and a Streamlit dashboard presents the index, trends and evidence.

Where the gain comes from. FinBERT without fine-tuning finds only 21% of the negative sentences and 53% of the positive ones, and calls 91% of the neutral ones neutral: it treats most of this corpus as having no sentiment. A likely reason is that it was trained on formal financial news, where a customer's complaint about a fee has no counterpart. The chosen model finds 59% of the negatives and 73% of the positives. It pays for this in neutral recall, which falls from 0.91 to 0.73. The negative class is small, 39 test sentences, so its figures are the least certain in the table. Accuracy barely moves because the test set is 56% neutral, so a model that says "neutral" often is rewarded; macro-F1, which weighs the three classes equally, shows the change.

Which step earned it. Each of the three changes can be read off the table. Fine-tuning on people's labels adds 0.050 macro-F1 over no fine-tuning. Moving to the LLM's labels adds 0.010, within one seed's standard deviation. That step changed two things at once, the labels and the rows (1,563 from four rounds in place of the first round's alone), so it shows only that the two together did no harm on people's test labels. Pretraining on the corpus adds a further 0.018. Averaging three seeds adds 0.021 and, more usefully, removes the dependence on a lucky seed.

How sure. The lead of the adapted encoder over plain fine-tuning is small on the test rows (0.649 against 0.631, about one standard deviation between seeds). It was chosen because every measure agrees, not because any one gap is large: across 15 paired cross-validation folds it is ahead by 0.014 macro-F1, better in 11 of the 15 (paired t-test, p = 0.016). Against FinBERT without fine-tuning the lead is about four standard deviations. Three further techniques were tested on the chosen model and changed nothing: class weights, source weights and both together moved cross-validated macro-F1 by -0.002 to -0.004, none distinguishable from zero.

What the model still gets wrong. It is right on about seven sentences in ten, and weakest where the language is least formal: 68% on Reddit against 81% on earnings text. That matters for the index, where the share of sentences labelled negative rose from about a tenth to about a sixth when this model replaced FinBERT (section 4.4).

A limit to state, not to fix

The training labels follow the later retail rule (a fee or a perk carries sentiment); the 299 test labels were made before it, when such sentences were neutral. Some of the model's "errors" on negative and positive are therefore the rule, not the model. How many is not measured, because the test rows were deliberately not relabelled. Say this wherever negative precision is quoted. A human-labelled test set under the new rule would be needed to separate the two.

Why similarity fails. It is barely better than always answering "retail banking" (41.3% against 37.4%). FinBERT's averaged embeddings were never trained to place sentences on the same topic near each other; they carry a sentence's register strongly, so a casual question resembles other casual questions more than it resembles a formal sentence on the same subject. The same weakness appears in the chatbot's evidence search (section 4.5). A model that reads the sentence and the definitions does not have this problem, and the two local models, of different families, land within a point of each other, which suggests the ceiling is the task and the prompt, not the model.

What remains hard. The local model is right 73% of the time, against 84% for a much larger model. Counted in sentences, most of that gap is retail banking, where the larger model is right on 62 more, and "other", 49 more; on wealth the larger model is behind, by 19. The wealth aspect is a separate problem, one of definition. Under the wording the corpus was classified with, the local model called 176 of the 2,075 labelled sentences "wealth", and only 63 of those are wealth by the rule the team later fixed, judged against the LLM rater's labels (precision 0.36).

Figures will change

A trial wrote the new wealth rule into the model's prompt. Against the labels made under that rule, agreement rose from 75.5% to 79.4% (148 sentences newly right, 67 newly wrong, exact McNemar p < 0.001); wealth precision rose from 0.36 to 0.70 while wealth recall fell from 0.76 to 0.46 (docs/evidence/wealth-definition-trial-2026-10-12.md). The whole corpus is being re-classified under the new wording. If the owner approves a second re-score, add the trial to this section as the third method and replace every per-aspect figure in Table 7.

Per-aspect figures made with similarity matching, with the current aspects, and after a second re-score are three different assignments. Quote only the final set, and never compare across them.

4.3Bank recognition

On the hard cases the recogniser is right where alias matching is right about four times in five. On a sheet of 201 sentences weighted towards misleading names, it names exactly the right banks for all 201, against 161 for alias matching (F1 0.89). Alias matching fails mainly in one way: it counts mentions of UOB Kay Hian, a separate brokerage, as UOB, and is right on only 3 of the 30 sentences that name it. It also misses some sentences that name OCBC only through Bank of Singapore or Great Eastern.

The perfect score overstates what to expect on new text. The sheet was built from the cases known to be hard, and the recogniser's last rules were written after reading its errors on this same sheet, so it is a check that the rules do what was intended, not a held-out test. Before those last rules it was right on 200.

On the whole corpus the two methods disagree on 258 of the 45,844 sentences, under 1%. In 145 of them alias matching says UOB and the recogniser says no bank, nearly all UOB Kay Hian. So the choice matters little for the headline and a good deal for a reader who searches for evidence about UOB and is shown a brokerage's stock note.

Sources: python -m train.rater_labels extra score ner and python -m preprocess.bank_ner corpus-diff, both on the submitted code and database; the sheet's design in docs/evidence/bank-ner-2026-10-01.md. The reference answers on the 201-sentence sheet are the LLM rater's, accepted by the team in place of hand labels.

The aspect method does not move the headline; the model does. With the new aspects and FinBERT's scores, every headline is identical to the old one, because a headline counts each sentence once and FinBERT scores a sentence the same whatever its aspect. This separates the two changes cleanly: everything in Table 6 is the model's effect. It changed the label of 13,154 of the 45,844 sentences (29%), and 10,219 of those were sentences FinBERT had called neutral.

Reddit drives the rise. FinBERT called 88% of Reddit sentences neutral; the chosen model calls 57% neutral, about a quarter positive and a sixth negative. Reddit is 56% of the corpus, so this one source moves the blend, and it explains why UOB gains most: 72% of UOB's sentences are from Reddit, against 49% of DBS's. Earnings text, the formal language FinBERT was built for, moves least: DBS is unchanged (0.286 to 0.287) and OCBC and UOB fall a little (0.375 to 0.344 and 0.406 to 0.335). CNA news falls for all three banks by 0.14 to 0.18, to below zero.

What a reader can and cannot conclude. The order of the banks changed: OCBC led DBS by 0.014, and DBS now leads by 0.012. A change of model reversed it, so the order of those two is not a finding. No confidence interval has been computed for any index score, and the only measured uncertainty is the model's error on about three sentences in ten; how that carries into a score over thousands of sentences is not known. The index supports "all three are mildly positive, with UOB lowest under both models" and does not support a ranking of DBS against OCBC. The per-source figures are more informative than the blend: each bank scores between 0.29 and 0.34 on its own earnings text and near zero on Reddit (0.00 to 0.07), a gap between what banks say and what customers say that the blend hides.

4.5The chatbot

On the re-scored database the chatbot chooses the right tool on 23 of 24 questions, declines advice and admits missing data on 8 of 8, and gave no invented citation in 32 answers. The last of these was reached by a check in code, not by the prompt.

Why a check in code. Before the checks, a third of answers (11 of 32) cited evidence that no search had returned: invented sentence numbers, links to news sites, quotes attributed to publications. None of those 11 answers had searched at all; each looked up a score and then wrote a "supporting evidence" section anyway. The prompt held two rules in conflict: one asked for that section in every answer, another forbade it without a search, and the model followed the first. Rewording the prompt might have helped, and was not tested by itself. The team chose a fix that does not depend on the model's obedience: any quote that no search returned is removed in code. That ended the problem without changing which tools the model chose. The same reasoning lies behind the intent classifier: advice and off-topic handling are reliable because those questions never reach the model.

What the checks cannot see. They verify quotes and numbers, not the model's own descriptions. In the latest run one answer was wrong in ways the quote and figure checks do not read (it is also the one miss on tool choice): asked for a bank's sentiment "in the last 7 days" on a topic with too few recent sentences, the model reported the all-time figure as if it were the week's, and gave the wrong year for the data. Both numbers were ones a tool had returned. With one aspect per sentence, thin recent cells are more common, so this failure will occur more often than it did. It is the main reason the automated results are a floor for trust, not a measure of faithfulness.

Evidence search. One aspect per sentence made the search for evidence far more precise. Asked what people say about a bank's topic, about four of the five sentences returned are ones people labelled with that topic (precision 0.78), where it was fewer than two (0.33) when a sentence carried six aspects. Most of that gain is the stored aspect acting as a filter: a random five from the filtered pool are already right 72% of the time. Ranking the pool by the aspect's name adds six points to that; ranking it by the question's own words adds none (0.69), which fits the weakness described in section 4.2, where the embeddings match a sentence's register more than its topic. The measure rests on 28 queries.

4.6Engineering checks

The system is tested as software as well as evaluated as a model. A fresh clone runs 2,520 automated tests with no network access. Forty-six of them are expected failures kept on purpose: each records a known limit of the chatbot's quote and figure checks, found in thirteen rounds of adversarial review, so that a limit cannot be forgotten or silently change. The API's index endpoints are tested to return the stored index unchanged and to reject an invalid bank, window or period with an error.

5Conclusions

For the team to write

The conclusions are the team's judgement and must be written by its members after the figures freeze. What follows is a draft of what the evidence supports today, to argue with. Keep each claim no stronger than its figure, and add what the team would do differently.

The index is usable as a mild, comparative signal, not as a ranking. The fine-tuned model is right on about seven sentences in ten, the three banks' headline scores sit within 0.04 of each other, and no interval has been computed for any of them. The system can show that all three are viewed mildly positively, that customers are far cooler than the banks' own reports, and which topics lift or drag each bank. It cannot say which of DBS and OCBC is viewed better.

The hardest problem was the neutral boundary, and it is a problem of definition before it is one of modelling. People, the LLM rater and every model disagree most on whether a sentence carries sentiment at all. The team had to rule on it twice. The wealth ruling had a large measured effect: only 36% of what the aspect model had called wealth fits it. The retail ruling changed few labels in practice, about 60 of 1,277, no more than two runs of the same rater differ by, which says more about how unstable the boundary is than about the rule. Neither question could have been settled by a larger model.

General-purpose embeddings failed twice, and a small language model fixed both. Similarity between FinBERT embeddings was barely better than a constant guess at assigning aspects (41% against 37%) and, before sentences had one stored aspect to filter on, no better than random at ranking evidence for a question. The embeddings encode how a sentence is written more than what it is about. Replacing similarity with a local model's reading of the sentence raised aspect agreement to 73% and, through the stored aspect, evidence precision from 0.33 to 0.78.

A language model in a product needs checks that do not depend on its obedience. With instructions alone, a third of answers cited evidence that was never retrieved; removing unsearched quotes in code ended that. The remaining errors are in the model's own wording, which no text check verifies, so the human ratings of section 4.5 are needed before any claim about faithfulness.

Limits that bound every claim above. The training labels are an LLM's, tested against people's labels made under an older rule. The test sets are 299 and 100 sentences. Bank attribution in the stored corpus is by alias matching. The corpus ends on 25 September 2026 and over-represents DBS in the banks' own text. With more time, the most valuable step would be a second human-labelled test set under the current rules, because it would turn the largest unmeasured uncertainty, how much of the model's disagreement with the test labels is the rule, into a number.

What it did well. It found defects that human review had missed, among them an earnings extractor that silently produced nothing because an exception was swallowed, and a chat tab that never sent conversation history to the model. It made evaluation cheap enough to repeat: every model was re-run under one protocol when the labelling rules changed. And it kept a written record of every decision and its evidence.

What went wrong, and how it was caught. Its text is plausible whether or not it is true. During the project it drew a confident conclusion from a comparison it had misread, let 63 unwanted items into the database through a filter that silently failed, and wrote a sentence attributing one bank's lead to the wrong source. Each was caught by a rule the team imposed, that a number counts only if a command run in the same session produced it, and by audits before data was relied on. The verification, not the fluency, is what makes the documentation trustworthy.

Where people stayed in control. The team chose the theme, the sources and the labelling rules, and decided the aspects, the scope of bank recognition and which model the product serves from options put to it. Deletions of data, a rewrite of the repository's history and the re-score of the corpus each waited for a team member's approval, the last after a rehearsal on a copy. A free-tier hosted model was limited to the live chatbot once the team learned its inputs could be reviewed by people.
